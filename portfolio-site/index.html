§1 How I got here

The short version is on my résumé. This is the real one.

  1. 2019 – 2023

    It started with labels

    My first real taste of data was an internship at ISRO’s Space Applications Centre, training models to find roads in satellite images. The model wasn’t the lesson. The labels were. Something as simple as how an image is labelled decided whether the whole thing worked.

    My thesis on meta-learning (MAML), and all the computer vision and machine learning that came with my degree at IIST, kept teaching me the same thing: understand the data, clean it, and squeeze every bit of information out of it before you run a single model on it.

  2. 2023 – 2024

    The hard year

    I wanted depth, so I wanted a master’s. And I wanted the US: the tech hub, and a shot at working for the companies I’d heard about my whole life.

    The year I spent applying was the hardest one I’ve had. Things were tough at home, and the job offers weren’t coming. So I didn’t wait. I published two research papers to make my profile stronger, and I taught myself to trade.

  3. Dec 2023 – Dec 2024

    Learning the market by trading it

    I traded EUR/USD, USD/JPY, USD/CAD, gold, silver and oil. Every day I read the higher timeframes, then executed on the lower ones, mostly around the London and New York opens.

    Every strategy went through the same loop: backtest it, trade it live, collect the data, then look hard at win rate, maximum drawdown, what failed and how often I passed. I kept adjusting my execution, my analysis and how I read market and news sentiment until the numbers held. That’s how I passed prop-firm challenges at Funding Pips, hitting a 15% profit target three times in a row without ever breaking the 10% total or 5% daily loss limit.

  4. 2025 – 2026

    Boston, and learning it from the base

    At Boston University I wanted to learn how it’s really done: how quant researchers and traders at banks and quant firms build strategies, and what model and risk analysts check before any money moves. With Prof. Eugene Pinsky I went back to the markets I’d traded, first on free data, then on exchange vendor data, then on Bloomberg.

    I turned the math into code and learned the market’s realities the honest way. A great Sharpe ratio in one period can fall apart in the next. A Newey-West t-test, a deflated Sharpe ratio and a maximum drawdown each tell you something different. And which data vendor you trust can change the answer completely. On a free feed, a 15-year pattern in the U.S. Dollar Index looked like money. On exchange data it disappeared. Proving why became my first-author paper in Risks.

    Research was only half of it. My coursework covered machine learning, big data analytics, data mining, algorithms, graph analytics and data visualization, and the class projects grew into real work. WikiFlow started as my big data term project and was published by Springer. My data science class project became the readmission study. A team project on graph partitioning became the MBTA resilience simulator.

    Along the way I taught machine learning to 60+ graduate students as a teaching assistant and led a six-person team to a podium finish at the BU MedAI Hackathon.

  5. Now

    What I want to do next

    My first choice is finance: quant research, trading analytics or risk at a bank, a quant firm or an asset manager, where data quality and honest testing decide real money.

    The same habits work anywhere, though, and I’d be just as glad to join any company as a data analyst, data engineer, data scientist or researcher. Either way, I bring work that already runs on its own: an AWS pipeline that won’t publish until 17 checks pass, a hospital call list that reaches 3× more readmissions than random calls, and an app that catches AI agents reaching the right answer the wrong way.

Does a fifteen-year pattern in the dollar survive a second data source?

Two blind spots in backtest validation

Problem
A strategy on the U.S. Dollar Index showed a 15-year overnight pattern, and standard overfitting tests rated it robust. Before anyone puts money on a result like that, someone has to find out whether it’s real.
What I did
Rebuilt the test on independently sourced data: ICE Dollar Index futures and five CME currency futures from Barchart, plus Federal Reserve spot and interest rates from FRED. Rebuilt the spot index from its six currencies with ICE’s formula. Wrote a one-line screen that flags bad opening prices, and a search engine that reproduces the original backtest exactly on all 1,312 configurations, about 17× faster, so the whole search could be rerun 2,000 times against luck. Research assistantship with Prof. Eugene Pinsky.
Tools
Python (pandas, NumPy, statsmodels), Excel for the first pass, SQL for the published dashboard. Newey-West, White’s Reality Check, Hansen’s SPA, CSCV.
Result
The pattern lived only in the free feed’s opening prices: −1.36 there, +0.25 on futures and +0.22 on the rebuilt spot index. Published as first author in Risks (October 2026). The same screen flagged 10 faulty series out of 37 price feeds before they reached any analysis.

Who should a care team call first after a diabetic patient goes home?

Readmission risk, re-analysed and put to work

Problem
About 1 in 9 diabetic patients in this data came back to hospital within 30 days. Care teams can’t call everyone, and a published 2026 benchmark set the bar for predicting who will return at AUC 0.664.
What I did
Re-ran the benchmark under the same nested, patient-grouped cross-validation; rebuilt the patient cohort and added patient-history features; tested two Transformer models against gradient boosting; audited accuracy by age, race and sex; turned the predictions into a call-list dashboard a care team can size to its capacity. It started as my class project in Data Science with Python; I later rebuilt it from scratch.
Tools
Python, LightGBM, XGBoost, scikit-learn, Tableau, HTML and JavaScript dashboard.
Result
AUC 0.689 against the published 0.664 on 101,766 encounters. Calling the riskiest 5% reaches 1,696 future readmissions, 3.0× what random calls reach. The simpler model shipped because the Transformers lost at up to 65 times the compute. The audit found no gap by race or sex, but a drop from 0.73 to 0.63 across age bands, which argues for age-aware thresholds.

Can you tell who’ll quit Wikipedia from their first ten edits?

WikiFlow: editor dropout at scale

Problem
Wikipedia loses most new editors; in the test set, 64.8% dropped out. Mentors need to know whom to reach first, from very little history.
What I did
Processed five years of English Wikipedia history on a 5-node Google Cloud Dataproc cluster. Engineered 13 features from each editor’s first ten edits and threw out co-editor graph features because they leaked the answer. Wrote logistic regression from scratch on Spark RDDs, distributed a Keras network with Elephas, and clustered newcomers into types. Built as my Big Data Analytics term project with Kunj Patel, then published with Prof. Farshid Alizadeh-Shabdiz.
Tools
PySpark, GCP Dataproc, BigQuery, Keras with Elephas.
Result
About 60 million revision events from 1,158,248 editors (25 GB compressed) for roughly $2.50 a full run. AUC 0.909 with the neural net, 0.845 in 19 seconds with the from-scratch model. Four kinds of newcomer. Published by Springer.

Which single closure hurts Boston’s subway most?

MBTA network resilience

Problem
Which subway stations, if closed, cut Boston’s network apart? The public schedule data had defects that dropped the Green Line and broke the map into six pieces.
What I did
Rebuilt the MBTA’s Spring 2025 schedule (20.7K trips, 355K stop-time records) into one connected network after fixing three pipeline defects. Compared Louvain, spectral and METIS partitioning across 25 seeds, simulated every closure, and shipped an interactive simulator. It began as a three-person class project that I later rebuilt and extended.
Tools
Python, NetworkX, JavaScript, GitHub Actions.
Result
125 stations and 127 segments with only three independent loops, so 110 of 125 stations are single points of failure. Closing Copley alone cuts off 56.1% of station pairs. Try it on the map: the numbers are computed live in your browser.

Can you trust an AI judge to grade an AI agent?

JudgeGuard: auditing agent traces and LLM judges

Problem
Teams use AI models to grade AI agents. But an agent can reach the right final answer through a wrong step, and an AI judge can score a wrong answer as highly as a right one.
What I did
Built an evaluation workbench that replays an agent’s tool calls and flags the exact step that doesn’t add up, and tests AI judges against source-supported answers with a real local model. Shipped it with continuous integration, property-based tests and a hardened Docker image.
Tools
Python, FastAPI, Gradio on Hugging Face ZeroGPU, Qwen3, Docker, GitHub Actions, Hypothesis.
Result
Live on Hugging Face. A Qwen3-4B judge ranked the supported answer first on 96 of 100 pairs (95% interval 92.45–99.03%), with all 200 raw responses published. The guard path meets a 150 ms p99 latency budget. Try the demo’s opening case on the right.

Should commodity investors chase last month’s winners?

Commodity rotation in futures and ETFs

Problem
Should commodity investors rotate into last period’s winners, and does the answer change between futures and ETFs?
What I did
Tested winner, median and loser rotation on the 15 most liquid Bloomberg commodity futures (2008–2026) and six commodity ETFs, rebalancing weekly to yearly, against equal weight, random portfolios, BCOM and DBC. Corrected for the number of strategies tried and measured each one’s break-even trading cost. With Prof. Eugene Pinsky.
Tools
Python, Bloomberg total-return indices, random-portfolio benchmarks, White’s Reality Check, Hansen’s SPA.
Result
Same rule, opposite answers. In ETFs, monthly winners turned $100 into $337, against $121 for equal weight and $86 for DBC. In futures, winners trailed equal weight at every horizon from a month to a year. Weekly rotation breaks even at only 4–5 basis points. Under review at Commodities.

How wrong is the bell curve when markets crash?

Multi-asset risk engine

Problem
Many risk models assume returns follow a bell curve. In a crash, that assumption can understate the loss a desk should plan for.
What I did
Wrote a multi-asset risk engine over commodities, the S&P 500, five CME currency futures and dollar-index futures (December 2010 to December 2025). Fitted GARCH and DCC models by maximum likelihood, compared Monte Carlo and block bootstrap, and backtested 8 VaR models the way a bank would.
Tools
Python, SciPy, GARCH and DCC, Monte Carlo, Kupiec and Christoffersen backtests, pytest.
Result
The normal model understated one-month 99% expected shortfall by about a third (11.1% vs 7.4%). Over 2,796 days, only DCC-GARCH with an empirical tail passed the full conditional-coverage test, while holding about 13% less VaR than historical simulation. On a 40/30/30 book, 52% of the risk came from the 40% equity weight.
